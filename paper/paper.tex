\begin{abstract}
We show that a widely cited class of complex-vs-real neural-network
performance gaps is, in part, a hyperparameter-selection artifact.
On a controlled 3-class RadioML 2018.01A subset, a 22.94~pp
matched-shared-trial gap under \code{crelu} collapses by
9.3$\times$ to 2.46~pp under independent per-family tuning, on the
same data and the same 16-trial search box. Per-step gradient
telemetry traces the difference to an explosion-into-dead-region
failure of real baselines at the high-learning-rate first AdamW step,
a regime the complex family tolerates because its
$(\mathrm{Re},\mathrm{Im})$ parameter coupling distributes the loss
signal across more readout slots. A 2$\times$2 learning-rate / activation
factorial confirms the threshold is hyperparameter-driven, not
activation-driven.

The methodological finding is embedded in a representation-first study
of when complex-valued networks help at all. A complex sample
$z=x+iy=re^{i\theta}$ packages amplitude and phase into one number; a
real network can see it as Cartesian, polar, magnitude-only, or
phase-only coordinates, and the right question is which mechanism
--- representation, the $U(1)$-equivariant subspace constraint of
complex multiplication, or optimization tolerance --- is doing the
work. On synthetic RF, PSK-only data favor the complex model (0.821 vs
0.333 for magnitude-only), QAM-only data favor magnitude, mixed PSK+QAM
gives a small complex edge, and unseen carrier-phase rotation breaks
coordinate-dependent models unless augmentation is added. Two
application pilots support the same reading: in quantum wavefunctions,
momentum is invisible to $|\psi|$ but recoverable from phase; in EEG
analytic signals, phase locking, amplitude bursts, and phase-amplitude
coupling each select different coordinate views. We frame complex
activations through the Wirtinger calculus and a Liouville trilemma
(bounded, holomorphic, nonconstant --- pick two), and characterize the
six standard activations against it. The paper's claim is
consequently scoped: complex-valued models are useful structured
inductive biases for phase- and amplitude-bearing data, but their
advantage is representation-, symmetry-, and optimization-dependent
--- and apparent CVNN-vs-real gaps should be reported under both
selection rules before being interpreted as architectural.
\end{abstract}

\section{First-principles setup}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{What a complex input contains}

A complex number is a two-dimensional real object with extra
structure. The same point can be written in Cartesian form
$z=x+iy$ or polar form $z=re^{i\theta}$, where
$r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ is magnitude and $\theta=\arg z$ is phase.
Equal magnitude does not mean equal information: $5$ and $3+4i$ both
have magnitude $5$, but their phases differ, their real parts differ,
and a model that only sees $|z|$ cannot distinguish them. Conversely,
if the label depends only on energy, phase may be a nuisance rather
than a signal.

This simple observation is the reason a complex-vs-real comparison can
be misleading. A real-valued network on $(x,y)$ sees the full
Cartesian information. A real network on $(r,\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$
sees magnitude and phase explicitly. A magnitude-only or phase-only
network sees a controlled information bottleneck. We use all of these
views because they answer different questions: whether information is
in amplitude, in phase, in their relation, or in the algebraic prior of
complex multiplication.

\subsection{What a complex layer constrains}

Complex multiplication is not an arbitrary two-channel real linear map.
Multiplying $(x+iy)$ by $(a+ib)$ gives
\[
  (a+ib)(x+iy) = (ax - by) + i(ay + bx),
\]
which is the real matrix
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}.
\]
This matrix is a rotation-and-scale coupling between the two real
channels. A generic real network can learn any $2\times2$ channel mix,
but the complex layer starts with this structured coupling built in.
The coupling is not arbitrary: as
\Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace} makes precise, complex layers are
exactly the $U(1)$-equivariant subspace of stacked-real $2$-channel
layers, parameterized by $2$ real numbers per tap instead of $4$. That
can help when the task respects phase geometry, and it can hurt or be
irrelevant when the task is amplitude-only or when a hand-chosen real
coordinate system already exposes the right variables.

The same distinction matters for invariance. If every input is rotated
by a common phase, $z\mapsto e^{i\varphi}z$, a bias-free complex
linear or convolutional layer rotates its output by the same phase.
This is equivariance, not magic invariance: biases, nonlinearities,
readout choices, and training data determine whether the final model
is actually robust to an unseen phase convention. Our fixed-rotation
stress tests are designed to catch this failure mode.

\subsection{Differentiation in the complex plane: Wirtinger calculus and an activation trilemma}
\label{sec:wirtinger-trilemma}

A common worry when first building a CVNN is that backpropagation
seems to require holomorphic activations, while Liouville's theorem
forbids any bounded entire nonlinearity. Both halves of that worry are
imprecise. The textbook treatment in
Hirose~\cite{hirose2012complex} and the more recent deep-CVNN survey
in Trabelsi et al.~\cite{trabelsi2018deep} resolve the issue
operationally by adopting split or magnitude-gated activations; we
restate the resolution here in the form most useful for the rest of
the paper. Backpropagation requires neither holomorphy nor even
complex-differentiability; what it requires is the standard Wirtinger
(CR-) calculus, in which every $\mathbb{R}$-differentiable map
$\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ has a well-defined gradient. The genuine
constraint Liouville imposes is then sharper than ``no good
activations exist'': it is a three-way trade-off between boundedness,
holomorphy, and nonconstancy, with phase equivariance as a fourth
desideratum that further constrains the design space. This subsection
makes both points precise, since they govern the rest of the paper.

\paragraph{Wirtinger derivatives.}
For $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ written in real coordinates as
$f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$ with $z=x+iy$, define
\[
  \partial_z f \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\partial_x f - i\,\partial_y f\bigr),
  \qquad
  \partial_{\bar z} f \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\partial_x f + i\,\partial_y f\bigr).
\]
Both derivatives exist whenever $f$ is real-differentiable as a map
$\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$. The Cauchy--Riemann equations are
exactly $\partial_{\bar z} f \equiv 0$, so $f$ is holomorphic on an
open set iff $\partial_{\bar z} f$ vanishes there. Holomorphy is a
\emph{constraint} on $f$, not a prerequisite for differentiating it.

\paragraph{Backpropagation does not require holomorphy.}
Let $L:\mathbb{C}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued loss and let
$w\in\mathbb{C}$ be a parameter. Treating $L$ as a function of the
real and imaginary parts of $w$, the steepest-descent direction in the
underlying $\mathbb{R}^2$ corresponds to the conjugate Wirtinger
gradient
\[
  \nabla_w L \;:=\; 2\,\overline{\partial_{w}L}
  \;=\; 2\,\partial_{\bar w} L,
\]
and the gradient update is $w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\partial_{\bar w}L$
(see~\cite{brandwood1983complex,kreutzdelgado2009complex} for
derivations). PyTorch's complex autograd stores
$\partial_{\bar w}L$ in the \code{.grad} field of complex parameters;
training proceeds with no holomorphy assumption. Activations such as
\code{CReLU} or \code{ZReLU}, which are nowhere holomorphic, are
nevertheless mathematically respectable inside this calculus: they are
real-differentiable almost everywhere, and their Wirtinger derivatives
exist in the same a.e.\ sense in which $\mathrm{ReLU}'$ exists on
$\mathbb{R}$.

\paragraph{The Liouville activation trilemma.}
The actual restriction Liouville imposes is the following.

\begin{proposition}[Activation trilemma]
\label{prop:trilemma}
No function $\sigma:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ can simultaneously be
\emph{(i)}~bounded on all of $\mathbb{C}$, \emph{(ii)}~entire
(holomorphic on all of $\mathbb{C}$), and \emph{(iii)}~nonconstant.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Liouville's theorem: a bounded entire function on $\mathbb{C}$ is
constant.\qed
\end{proof}

\noindent A useful complex activation must therefore drop at least one
of \emph{(i)--(iii)}. Each existing activation makes a different
choice, and the choice is the design variable rather than an
inconvenience to be hidden:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{ComplexTanh} ($\sigma(z)=\tanh z$) keeps holomorphy and
    nonconstancy but drops boundedness: it is meromorphic on
    $\mathbb{C}$ with simple poles at $z = i(\pi/2+k\pi)$, $k\in\mathbb{Z}$,
    and blows up arbitrarily close to those poles.
  \item \code{Siglog} ($\sigma(z)=z/(1+|z|)$) keeps boundedness and
    nonconstancy but drops holomorphy globally: it depends on $|z|$,
    so $\partial_{\bar z}\sigma\not\equiv 0$ on any open set.
  \item \code{CReLU}, \code{ZReLU}, \code{ModReLU}, and
    \code{ComplexCardioid} all drop holomorphy outright. They are
    $\mathbb{R}$-differentiable a.e., bounded only on bounded subsets
    of $\mathbb{C}$, and chosen for their interaction with magnitude
    and phase rather than for any analytic property.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A fourth desideratum: phase equivariance.}
For phase-bearing data the natural symmetry is the $U(1)$ action
$z\mapsto e^{i\varphi}z$. An activation respects this symmetry iff
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:phase-equivariance}
  \sigma(e^{i\varphi} z) \;=\; e^{i\varphi}\,\sigma(z)
  \qquad \forall\,\varphi\in\mathbb{R},\ z\in\mathbb{C}.
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:phase-equivariance} with holomorphy is more
restrictive than Liouville alone:

\begin{proposition}[Phase-equivariant entire functions are linear]
\label{prop:equivariant-entire}
If $\sigma:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ is entire and satisfies
\eqref{eq:phase-equivariance}, then $\sigma(z)=cz$ for some
$c\in\mathbb{C}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Expand $\sigma(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_n z^n$ on $\mathbb{C}$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:phase-equivariance} gives
$\sum_n a_n e^{in\varphi}z^n = \sum_n a_n e^{i\varphi} z^n$ for all
$\varphi$, hence $a_n(e^{in\varphi}-e^{i\varphi})=0$ for all
$\varphi\in\mathbb{R}$, which forces $a_n=0$ for $n\neq 1$.\qed
\end{proof}

So the four desiderata --- bounded, holomorphic, nonconstant,
phase-equivariant --- are jointly satisfiable only by the trivial
constant or linear maps. The six activations characterized in
\Cref{sec:activation-characterization} can be read as a natural
experiment over this $2\times 2\times 2\times 2$ taxonomy with the
useless cells removed: \code{ModReLU} and \code{Siglog} keep phase
equivariance at the cost of holomorphy; \code{ComplexTanh} keeps
holomorphy at the cost of boundedness; \code{CReLU}, \code{ZReLU},
and \code{ComplexCardioid} drop both holomorphy and equivariance to
buy other useful properties (split-channel sparsity, quadrant
selection, magnitude gating).

\paragraph{The CR-residual is the trilemma made measurable.}
The Cauchy--Riemann residual reported in
\Cref{fig:activation-tradeoff} is, up to a factor of two, exactly
$|\partial_{\bar z}\sigma|$ evaluated on a grid in $\mathbb{C}$. It is
zero on open sets where $\sigma$ is holomorphic and strictly positive
elsewhere, so it quantifies the deviation from holomorphy that
\Cref{prop:trilemma} forces every nonconstant bounded activation to
incur. Pairing CR-residual against gradient norm at initialization
turns the abstract trilemma into a per-activation diagnostic: an
activation either pays a holomorphy cost (positive CR residual) or a
boundedness cost (large intrinsic Jacobian), and the figure shows
each candidate's chosen trade.

With this calculus in place, the rest of the paper treats activation
choice as a structured design decision rather than a workaround for a
theorem that does not actually obstruct backpropagation.

\subsection{What this paper contributes}

The paper makes three contributions at a scale where the mechanisms
can be inspected:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A representation-first evaluation protocol.} We compare
    native complex models against real Cartesian, parameter-matched
    real, FLOP-matched real, polar, phase-only, and magnitude-only
    baselines. This separates ``complex arithmetic helped'' from
    ``the model was simply given the right coordinates.''
  \item \textbf{Stress tests across domains.} Synthetic RF, quantum
    wavefunctions, and EEG analytic signals show the same pattern:
    phase-bearing tasks reward phase-aware views; amplitude-heavy tasks
    reward magnitude; coupled phase/amplitude tasks reward joint
    representations; and unseen phase conventions require explicit
    testing or augmentation.
  \item \textbf{An optimization caution for RadioML.} On a 3-class
    RadioML subset, a large matched-shared-trial gap is mostly a
    high-learning-rate robustness artifact. Independent per-family
    tuning gives a much smaller gap, and gradient telemetry explains
    why.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The paper does \emph{not} claim:

\begin{itemize}
  \item That complex arithmetic is universally better than real-valued
    learning. The RF stress tests deliberately include cases where
    real magnitude or matched real baselines win. The contribution is
    to map the boundary of the advantage, not to erase the boundary.
  \item That CVNNs beat real-valued networks at full RadioML 2018.01A
    scale (24 modulations $\times$ 26 SNR levels $\times$ 1024-sample
    length). The infrastructure for that experiment is in place
    (\code{experiments/rf/sweep\_radioml.py --preset full}), but the
    run is deferred. The mechanism analysis is the contribution;
    scale-up is signposted future work.
  \item That the inductive bias survives in 2-D regimes (audio STFT,
    MRI). \code{ComplexConv2d} is not implemented; the project is 1-D
    only.
  \item That the quantum pilot establishes broad physics superiority.
    The quantum and EEG pilots are deliberately small application
    probes, included to test whether the representation thesis
    transfers outside RF.
  \item That CReLU activations generalize across signal-processing
    settings. The activation ablation
    (\Cref{sec:radioml-ablation}) is part of the contribution
    precisely because activation choice changes both the apparent size
    and, in one row, the direction of CVNN-vs-real gaps.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:framework}

\Cref{sec:scope} turns the motivation into three empirical questions:
what information is present, what algebraic constraint the model
imposes, and how optimization changes under that constraint. The
experimental design keeps these questions separate.

\paragraph{Coordinate views.}
For any complex sequence $z_t=x_t+i y_t$, the same sample can be
presented as Cartesian channels $(x_t,y_t)$, polar channels
$(|z_t|,\cos\theta_t,\sin\theta_t)$, phase-only channels
$(\cos\theta_t,\sin\theta_t)$, or magnitude-only $|z_t|$. These views
are not weaker or stronger in a single ordering. They encode different
hypotheses: phase-only should win when labels depend on angle or phase
lag; magnitude-only should win when labels depend on energy; polar
should win when amplitude and phase interact; native complex layers
should help when the rotation-and-scale coupling of complex
multiplication matches the task geometry.

\paragraph{Model families.}
Each benchmark compares the native complex network with real-valued
baselines that control for different confounds: a Cartesian real
network on stacked real/imaginary channels, a parameter-matched real
network, a FLOP-matched real network, and a rotation-equivariant real
reparameterization that constrains each $2$-channel kernel tap to the
$aI+bJ$ subspace of $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ identified by
\Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace}. The rotation-equivariant baseline is
provably equivalent to the complex layer (forward and gradient,
verified in \code{tests/test\_rotation\_equivariant.py}); we use it as
a correctness witness rather than a separate experimental row, and
discuss why the substantive comparison is therefore complex versus the
unconstrained Cartesian and parameter-matched baselines in
\Cref{sec:rotation-equivariant-control}. Architecture details and
parameter formulas are deferred to \Cref{app:architectures}; the main
point is that every reported win must survive at least one plausible
real-valued way of seeing the same information.

\paragraph{Nonlinearity in the complex plane.}
Activation choice is a first-principles issue rather than a software
detail. \Cref{sec:wirtinger-trilemma} sets up the Wirtinger calculus
that makes complex backpropagation well-defined and states the
trilemma (\Cref{prop:trilemma}) any nonconstant complex activation
must navigate; in this section we use that taxonomy to fix the
candidate set. We characterize six activations before using them
downstream: \code{CReLU}, \code{ZReLU},
\code{ModReLU}~\cite{arjovsky2016unitary}, \code{ComplexCardioid},
\code{Siglog}, and \code{ComplexTanh}. Together they cover the
non-degenerate cells of the trilemma --- bounded but non-holomorphic,
holomorphic but unbounded, and split-channel constructions that drop
both holomorphy and phase equivariance to gain other properties.

\paragraph{Stress-test factors.}
The RF synthetic suite varies the data-generating mechanism rather
than only the model: PSK-only, QAM-only, mixed PSK+QAM, low and high
SNR, unit-magnitude normalization, unit-power normalization, fixed
carrier-phase rotation, random-rotation augmentation, and activation
choice. The quantum and EEG pilots reuse the same logic in other
domains by constructing tasks where the label is known to live in
phase, magnitude, or a phase-amplitude relation.

\paragraph{Selection rules.}
Every model family draws from the same search space and consumes the
same trial-by-seed budget. The matched-shared-trial rule chooses the
complex reference trial by validation accuracy, then reports every
baseline at that same trial index; this is a stress test for robustness
under a shared hyperparameter allocation. Independent per-family
selection is also reported where it changes the interpretation,
because it estimates best-achievable performance when each family is
tuned separately.

\section{Experiments and results}
\label{sec:findings}

\subsection{Activation characterization}
\label{sec:activation-characterization}

The activation question is where complex-valued neural networks first
depart from the intuition of ordinary real-valued networks. A useful
activation must be nonlinear; a globally bounded holomorphic function
cannot be nonlinear. Practical CVNN activations therefore choose a
compromise. Some are locally analytic but can blow up; some are bounded
but explicitly non-holomorphic; some preserve global phase rotations
while others deliberately break them.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/activation_tradeoff.png}
  \caption{Liouville trade-off, made empirical. \emph{Left}: gradient
    norm at init for each candidate activation, log scale.
    \emph{Right}: median and p95 Cauchy--Riemann residual on a
    $[-3, 3]^2$ grid in $\mathbb{C}$, log scale. \code{complex\_tanh}
    is locally holomorphic but unbounded (poles at $\pm i\pi/2$ inside
    the grid); \code{siglog} is bounded but never holomorphic;
    \code{crelu}, \code{zrelu}, \code{modrelu}, and
    \code{complex\_cardioid} sit between, each making a different
    compromise.}
  \label{fig:activation-tradeoff}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:activation-tradeoff} makes the Liouville bind empirically
visible. \code{complex\_tanh} has near-zero CR residual at the median
--- it really is locally holomorphic --- but the same metric's max is
905, because the grid (extent $= 3$) brackets the first pair of poles
at $z = \pm i\pi/2 \approx \pm 1.5708\,i$. We emphasize that this
large residual is not a Cauchy--Riemann violation in the analytic
sense: $\tanh$ is holomorphic at every non-pole point, with
$\partial_{\bar z}\tanh \equiv 0$ exactly there. The plotted residual
is a finite-difference estimate of $|\partial_{\bar z}\tanh|$, and
finite differences inflate near singularities. The plot is honest as
an \emph{operational} diagnostic of activation behaviour on a fixed
grid (which is what training sees), but the trilemma cell
\code{complex\_tanh} occupies is ``meromorphic, unbounded'' rather
than ``never holomorphic.'' The intrinsic Jacobian mean is $\sim$108. By contrast, \code{siglog} is bounded ($|f| < 1$
everywhere) and never blows up, but its CR residual is everywhere
positive (p95 = 0.25) --- it is not holomorphic anywhere, by
construction. The remaining four activations sit between, each making
a different compromise.

This is not a finding about which activation is best in training ---
it is a visible measurement of the formal trade-off the project is
built around. The choice of activation in the downstream benchmarks
(default \code{crelu}, following the pragmatic choice in
Trabelsi et al.~\cite{trabelsi2018deep}) is consistent across runs
and isolated from the architectural variables. Our results in
\Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism} and \Cref{sec:lr-vs-activation} qualify the
\code{crelu} default in a way Trabelsi et al.\ did not have to
contend with: \code{crelu} interacts asymmetrically with
matched-shared-trial hyperparameter selection through its split-ReLU
readout sensitivity, while \code{zrelu} and \code{modrelu}
(the latter from Arjovsky et al.~\cite{arjovsky2016unitary} and
extended by Wisdom et al.~\cite{wisdom2016fullcapacity} as the
unitary-RNN nonlinearity) do not. The activation choice and the
selection rule cannot be discussed independently.

\subsection{Synthetic phase classification --- null result}
\label{sec:phase-classification}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/synthetic_phase_classification_swept.png}
  \caption{1-D phase classification: matched shared-trial test
    accuracy after a 16$\times$3 sweep. All four families converge to
    $\sim$76\% with overlapping CIs --- the complex inductive bias has
    nothing to bite on at this scope.}
  \label{fig:phase-swept}
\end{figure}

After a 16$\times$3 budgeted sweep, the matched shared-trial comparison
puts all four families at $\sim$76.2--76.7\% test accuracy with
overlapping CIs (\Cref{fig:phase-swept}). On 1-D phase classification,
\textbf{the complex inductive bias does not help.} A 2-D real network
on $(\mathrm{Re}, \mathrm{Im})$ already has access to all the
information the task contains; there is no temporal or geometric
structure for $\mathbb{C}$-multiplication to exploit. This is exactly
what the phase-equivariance analysis in \Cref{sec:phase-equivariance}
predicts: a single-sample phase task does not invoke any sequence-level
equivariance.

\subsection{Why no rotation-equivariant baseline row}
\label{sec:rotation-equivariant-control}

\Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace} characterizes complex layers as
exactly the $U(1)$-equivariant subspace of stacked-real $2$-channel
layers. Operationally this means the natural ``complex inductive bias,
implemented in real coordinates'' baseline --- a real Conv1d whose
kernel taps are constrained to $aI + bJ$, with channel-wise ReLU and a
magnitude readout --- is not a separate model; it is the same model as
\code{ComplexConv1d} with \code{CReLU} and $|\cdot|$ readout, modulo
the channel layout convention.

We make this concrete in \code{cvnn.baselines.rotation\_equivariant}
and the test suite \code{tests/test\_rotation\_equivariant.py}, which
asserts numerical equivalence of the forward pass and per-parameter
gradients under matched initialization (atol \mbox{$\leq 10^{-6}$})
and verifies that three AdamW optimization steps from a shared
self-supervised loss leave $(a, b)$ within $1.5 \times 10^{-8}$ of
$(\mathrm{Re}(W), \mathrm{Im}(W))$ --- the difference between
\code{float32} representations of the same trajectory. The empirical
``rotation-equivariant baseline ablation'' would therefore report
identical numbers to the complex row, by construction. We elect to
report the equivalence as a tested theorem rather than a redundant
table row.

The substantive comparison in \Cref{tab:synthetic-rf} is therefore
complex (equivalently, rotation-equivariant 2-parameter-per-tap real)
\emph{versus the unconstrained 4-parameter-per-tap stacked, matched,
and FLOP-matched real baselines}. The 3.8--4.5~pp matched-shared-trial
gap is the cost the unconstrained baselines pay for spending the
extra two parameters per tap on the asymmetric complement of
$\iota(\mathbb{C})$ --- parameters that, if the task respects $U(1)$,
are at best wasted and at worst overfit the asymmetry. Read this way,
the win is not ``complex arithmetic helped''; it is ``the
$U(1)$-equivariant 2-parameter subspace generalizes better than its
4-parameter superset on PSK-like data,'' which is exactly what
\Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace} predicts.

\subsection{RF representation stress tests --- where the claim breaks}
\label{sec:representation-stress}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering

The matched-budget result in \Cref{sec:synthetic-rf} answers one
question: under a conventional Cartesian real baseline, does the
complex conv stack help? It does. But it does not answer the more
important mechanism question: is the advantage due to native complex
arithmetic, phase information, amplitude information, or a coordinate
choice that a real network could also receive? The representation
stress tests in \Cref{fig:rf-stress-heatmap} make those alternatives
explicit.

Three conclusions follow. First, PSK-only modulation is the cleanest
case for the complex prior: magnitude-only is exactly chance while
phase-aware real views and complex models learn. Second, the same
statement is false for QAM-only data: amplitude carries class
structure, and magnitude-only is the best single view in the committed
stress test. Third, native complex layers are not automatically
invariant to a carrier-phase offset: fixed rotation at test time drops
all coordinate-dependent PSK models to chance-level behavior, and
random rotation augmentation only partially restores performance.
This is the key scope refinement. The paper should not claim
``complex beats real'' as a universal result; it should claim that
complex-valued models are a useful inductive bias for RF signal
learning when the task's phase/amplitude geometry and invariance
assumptions match the model.

\subsection{Quantum wavefunction pilot --- physics application}
\label{sec:quantum-pilot}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/physics_quantum_accuracy_heatmap.png}
  \caption{1-D quantum wavefunction pilot. The momentum task stores the
    label in the phase gradient of $\psi(x)$, so magnitude-only stays
    at chance while phase-aware views solve it. The potential-inverse
    tasks are above chance but do not give a universal complex win:
    explicit real coordinate systems match or beat the complex model,
    and global-phase robustness must be tested directly.}
  \label{fig:quantum-pilot}
\end{figure}

To test whether the same representation story transfers outside RF, we
added a small physics pilot over 1-D complex wavefunctions. The data
are synthetic but physically grounded. In the first task, examples are
Gaussian wavepackets
$\psi(x)=A(x)\exp(i k x + i\phi_0)$ and the model predicts one of four
momentum classes. The amplitude envelope is randomized independently
of class, so the class information is in the phase gradient rather
than in $|\psi|$. In the second task, a right-moving wavepacket is
evolved under the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation with a
split-step Fourier integrator, and the model predicts one of five
potential families: free, barrier, well, double barrier, or harmonic.
We also evaluate the potential task under an unseen global phase
rotation $\psi\mapsto i\psi$ and with random global-phase augmentation
at train time.

\Cref{tab:quantum-pilot} gives the useful scope boundary. First,
phase can be indispensable in a physics state: all phase-aware or
Cartesian views reach 1.000 on momentum classification, while
magnitude-only is exactly chance. This is the quantum analogue of the
PSK result. Second, the potential-inverse task is learnable above
chance, but the best model is the explicit real Cartesian baseline
(0.462) rather than the complex model (0.431). Third, global phase is a
physical nuisance symmetry, but the current complex conv stack does not
automatically solve it: under an unseen $\pi/2$ phase shift, complex
drops to 0.323 while magnitude remains unchanged at 0.331. Random
global-phase augmentation partially recovers the complex model
(0.395), but does not make this a solved invariance problem. The
physics pilot therefore strengthens the paper's positioning: complex
models are plausible for domains with native complex fields, but the
right claim is still representation- and symmetry-conditional.

At $-10$~dB everyone is near chance ($\sim$33\% for 3 classes); from

\subsubsection{Comparison to published RadioML numbers}

The RadioML 2018.01A dataset paper~\cite{oshea2018radioml} reports a
real-valued ResNet-style classifier reaching roughly 90\% top-1 at
high SNR on the full 24-class task with the full sample length 1024.
Subsequent CVNN-on-RadioML
work~\cite{krzyston2020complex,tu2020complex} reports a typical
complex-vs-real advantage of a few percentage points on the same
24-class setup with comparable architectures. Our $+3$-and-flat
ablation result on \code{modrelu} / \code{zrelu} (the activations under
which real baselines are also stable) is consistent with that small
reported gap. The 22--23~pp gap on \code{crelu} / \code{cardioid} /
\code{siglog} is \emph{not} a contradiction of the literature --- it
is the matched-shared-trial selection rule magnifying real-baseline
instability that the literature's separate-tuning protocols would not
expose.

\subsubsection{Mechanism --- explosion-into-dead-region at step 1}
\label{sec:radioml-mechanism}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/headline_dead_seed_mechanism.png}
  \caption{Per-step trajectories on synthetic AWGN. \emph{Top row:}
    head.weight gradient (log scale). \emph{Bottom row:} train loss
    with the 3-class chance threshold ($\ln 3$) overlaid. Under
    \code{crelu}@matched (left), three real-baseline seeds spike
    above $\|\nabla\| \sim 10$ at step~1, then collapse to
    $\sim 10^{-6}$ within five steps and never recover (loss pinned
    at $\ln 3$). Under \code{zrelu}@matched (right) the same
    families train smoothly. The mechanism is local to the high-learning-rate
    AdamW first step, not the rest of training.}
  \label{fig:mechanism-trajectories}
\end{figure}

\Cref{sec:radioml-ablation} reports an asymmetry without explaining
it. We re-ran the matched-shared-trial selected configuration with
per-step instrumentation (train loss, total parameter gradient norm,
per-parameter gradient norm, max parameter magnitude) for each of
$5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60$ runs, capped at 200 steps (the divergence
pattern appears within the first 5).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/radioml_telemetry_grad.png}
  \caption{RadioML telemetry --- total gradient norm post-backward,
    log $y$-axis, one panel per activation. Real-baseline spikes at
    step 1 under \code{crelu} / \code{cardioid} / \code{siglog} mark
    the explosion-into-dead-region failure mode that produces the
    inflated gaps in \Cref{tab:ablation-summary}; under \code{modrelu}
    and \code{zrelu} no such spikes occur and the gap collapses to
    $\pm 3$~pp.}
  \label{fig:telemetry-grad}
\end{figure}

The mechanism is unambiguous (\Cref{fig:telemetry-grad}). On
\code{crelu} / \code{cardioid} / \code{siglog}, matched-shared-trial
picks a high-learning-rate / wide-hidden config that complex selects because it
tolerates it (learning rate $\in \{0.024, 0.024, 0.040\}$, hidden = 64). At
step~1, every family sees a large AdamW step away from random init.
For real baselines this produces a step-1 train loss in $[3, 36]$, a
total gradient norm in $[6, 42]$, and a \code{head.weight} gradient in
$[6, 41]$ --- i.e.\ nearly all of the explosion sits on the final
classifier's weights. For the complex network the same step produces a
step-1 loss in $[1.07, 1.28]$, total gradient norm $[0.3, 1.0]$,
\code{head.weight} gradient $[0.1, 0.7]$ --- about 50--80$\times$
smaller than its real counterparts on identical data and learning rate.

The complex network's $(\mathrm{Re}, \mathrm{Im})$ parameter coupling
distributes the loss signal across more parameter slots, so the same
$\mathrm{lr} \times \mathrm{CE\text{-}gradient}$ product produces a
smaller effective step on \code{head.weight}. The real network does
not have that smoothing; AdamW's first step pushes some seeds into a
region where the second \code{Conv1d}'s ReLU activations are all-zero
(or all-active and saturated), gradients vanish, and training
``stabilizes'' at uniform-prediction chance level. We confirmed this:
\emph{the dead seeds end at exactly $\mathrm{loss} = \ln 3 \approx
1.099$} (3-class CE under uniform predictions), with
\code{total\_grad\_norm} $\leq 0.05$, and zero test-accuracy
improvement from random init.

\Cref{tab:dead-seeds} summarizes the dead-seed counts. \textbf{The
dead-seed rate jumps from 0\% to 33\% as soon as the
matched-shared-trial selected learning rate crosses $\sim$0.02.} Two orders of
magnitude separation in the step-1 head gradient between the unstable
and stable activation regimes.

\subsubsection{Learning-rate-vs-activation disambiguation}
\label{sec:lr-vs-activation}

\Cref{tab:dead-seeds} reads as if some activations
(\code{crelu}, \code{cardioid}, \code{siglog}) are intrinsically
unstable and others (\code{modrelu}, \code{zrelu}) are intrinsically
stable. A skeptical reading is that the same data is just as
consistent with the dead-seed pattern being driven by the
\emph{learning rate} that matched-shared-trial selects, not the
activation: the unstable rows all selected learning rates in
$[0.024, 0.040]$, the stable rows in $[0.0024, 0.008]$. To
disambiguate, we ran a 2$\times$2 factorial on synthetic AWGN data:
\{\code{crelu}, \code{zrelu}\} $\times$
\{learning rate $=0.0236$, learning rate $=0.0024$\}, holding all
other hyperparameters at each activation's matched-shared-trial
selection. Three real families $\times$ three seeds per cell, capped
at 200 steps.

\Cref{tab:lr-vs-activation} reports the disambiguation.
\textbf{Learning rate is the dominant axis.} Dropping \code{crelu}
from its matched learning rate (0.0236) to \code{zrelu}'s learning
rate (0.0024) eliminates dead seeds entirely
($4/9 \to 0/9$) and shrinks the step-1 head gradient by an order of
magnitude ($31.4 \to 2.3$). Conversely, forcing \code{zrelu} to
\code{crelu}'s learning rate produces a step-1 head gradient of $6.3$ and the
first dead seed under that activation in this study ($1/9$). Both
activations cross from ``stable'' to ``unstable'' as learning rate crosses
$\sim$0.02 --- the threshold from \Cref{tab:dead-seeds} is hyperparameter-driven,
not activation-driven, and replicates the
\Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism} mechanism with the activation factor
swapped in.

\textbf{Activation is a secondary modulator.} At the same high learning rate,
\code{crelu} produces a step-1 head gradient $\sim$5$\times$ larger
than \code{zrelu} ($31.4$ vs $6.3$) and four times the dead-seed rate
($4/9$ vs $1/9$); the split-ReLU structure of \code{crelu} amplifies
the readout's response to a high-learning-rate first step but does not by itself
trigger the failure mode at safe learning rates. The honest reading
of \Cref{tab:dead-seeds} is therefore not ``some activations are
unstable and others are stable'' but ``the matched-shared-trial rule
selected unsafe learning rates for some activations and safe ones
for others, and that learning-rate difference --- modulated by activation
sensitivity --- is what drives the dead-seed counts.''

\subsection{The matched-shared-trial selection artifact}
\label{sec:selection-rules}

The activation ablation (\Cref{sec:radioml-ablation}), the per-step
mechanism (\Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism}), and the
learning-rate-vs-activation factorial (\Cref{sec:lr-vs-activation})
together support a methodological finding that is independent of any
particular complex-vs-real comparison: \emph{the matched-shared-trial
hyperparameter selection rule, used widely in CVNN benchmarking to
``hold capacity fixed across families,'' can manufacture
order-of-magnitude accuracy gaps that vanish under independent
per-family selection.} On our RadioML 3-class subset under

A 9.3$\times$ collapse in the headline gap is not a small bookkeeping
effect. The mechanism in \Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism} and
\Cref{sec:lr-vs-activation} explains why the
two rules diverge so sharply on this task: matched-shared-trial picks
the validation-best trial \emph{for the complex family} and forces the
real baselines to evaluate at that index. When the complex family's
best trial happens to use a learning rate above the
explosion-into-dead-region threshold ($\sim$0.02 here), the real
baselines get pushed through a regime that complex tolerates --- via
its $(\mathrm{Re},\mathrm{Im})$ parameter coupling on the readout ---
and they do not. Independent selection lets each family avoid that
regime, and the apparent advantage shrinks to a few percentage points.

\paragraph{What we recommend.}
The two rules answer different questions. Matched-shared-trial answers
``under a shared hyperparameter allocation, which family is more robust
to the chosen regime?'' Independent per-family answers ``under
separate tuning, which family attains the higher peak performance?''
Both are legitimate, neither subsumes the other, and reporting only
one risks either overstating the gap (matched only) or erasing a real
but small effect (independent only). Concretely, we recommend that
CVNN-vs-real comparisons report:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the matched-shared-trial result, framed as a robustness stress
    test rather than a peak-performance estimate;
  \item the independent per-family result as the
    best-achievable point estimate; and
  \item the dead-seed count under the matched rule, since the gap
    between the two rules is bounded below by what the matched rule's
    instability mechanism contributes.
\end{enumerate}
A complex-family victory that survives \emph{both} rules is a
substantively different claim from one that survives only the matched
rule. The CVNN literature, including our own earlier draft, has
historically reported only one or the other; the asymmetry between
them is itself a finding.

\paragraph{Scope of the artifact.}
The mechanism we measure is local to readouts that see
seed-dependent input scales (here, stacked $(\mathrm{Re},\mathrm{Im})$
features into a ReLU classifier head fed from a Cartesian conv stack)
under the matched-trial rule's tendency to select aggressive learning
rates that the complex family tolerates. We do not claim every
matched-shared-trial CVNN result in the literature is similarly
inflated. We do claim that, in the absence of either an independent
per-family rerun or a per-step gradient telemetry trace, a large
matched-trial gap is not safely interpretable as a complex-family
advantage. \Cref{sec:lr-vs-activation}, \Cref{app:telemetry}, and
\Cref{app:synthetic-replication}
give the diagnostic recipe; any future CVNN benchmark that adopts
matched-trial selection should run it.

\section{Interpretation}
\label{sec:interpretation}

The findings together support a more nuanced and falsifiable story
than the original ``complex wins'' framing:

\begin{quote}
  Complex-valued models should be viewed as structured inductive
  biases for phase- and amplitude-bearing data, not as universally
  stronger function approximators. They help when the task's geometry
  aligns with complex multiplication, they can be matched or beaten by
  explicit real coordinate systems in other regimes, and they change
  optimization tolerance under shared hyperparameter stress.
\end{quote}

This framing is deliberately application-facing. In RF engineering, the
question is rarely whether the samples are ``complex'' in a formal
sense; they are. The practical question is whether the deployment
setting needs a model that preserves phase geometry, extracts amplitude
geometry, withstands unknown carrier-phase rotations, or trains
robustly under limited tuning. The stress tests show that those are
separate axes. A PSK classifier, a QAM classifier, and a rotated
carrier-offset classifier should not be expected to reward the same
architecture. The application pilots make the same point outside RF:
wavefunction momentum is invisible to $|\psi|$ but explicit
Cartesian/polar coordinates can match native complex layers; EEG
analytic signals separate phase locking, amplitude bursts, and
phase-amplitude coupling into different winning representations.
In both cases, the relevant symmetry or coordinate view has to be
tested directly rather than assumed from the complex dtype.

\subsection{Why complex helps on IQ data --- phase equivariance}
\label{sec:phase-equivariance}

The intuition that ``complex multiplication has the right semantics
for phase-bearing signals'' can be made formal in one short lemma.

\begin{lemma}[Phase equivariance of \code{ComplexConv1d}]
  \label{lem:phase-equivariance}
  Let $C(z)[t] = \sum_{\tau} K[\tau] \cdot z[t-\tau] + b$ be a
  \code{ComplexConv1d} layer with complex-valued kernel $K$, complex
  bias $b$, and complex input sequence $z$. For any global phase
  $\varphi \in \mathbb{R}$ and any $t$,
  \[
    C(e^{i\varphi} \cdot z)[t]
    = e^{i\varphi} \cdot C(z)[t] + (1 - e^{i\varphi}) \cdot b.
  \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  By linearity of complex multiplication and convolution,
  \[
    C(e^{i\varphi} \cdot z)[t]
    = \sum_{\tau} K[\tau] \cdot (e^{i\varphi} \cdot z[t-\tau]) + b
    = e^{i\varphi} \sum_{\tau} K[\tau] \cdot z[t-\tau] + b
    = e^{i\varphi} \cdot \bigl(C(z)[t] - b\bigr) + b. \qedhere
  \]
\end{proof}

In the bias-free case (or after subtracting the bias) the result is
exactly equivariant. The biased case adds a fixed offset
$(1 - e^{i\varphi}) \cdot b$. If the intervening stack preserves the
global phase action, the head's $|\cdot|$ magnitude operation removes
it ($|e^{i\varphi} \cdot y| = |y|$ for real $\varphi$), yielding
phase-invariant logits. Our actual benchmarks include activations that
do \emph{not} preserve exact equivariance, so we treat this as an
architectural prior supplied by complex multiplication rather than a
guarantee that the trained network is globally phase-invariant.

The same does not hold for \code{Conv1d} operating on a stacked
$(\mathrm{Re}\,z, \mathrm{Im}\,z)$ real input. A global phase shift
$e^{i\varphi}$ corresponds to multiplying each $(I, Q)$ sample by the
$2 \times 2$ rotation matrix
\[
  R_\varphi \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\
  \sin\varphi & \phantom{-}\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}
  \;=\; \exp(\varphi J),
  \qquad
  J \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & \phantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Per-tap equivariance therefore requires every real conv kernel
$W_k\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$ to commute with $R_\varphi$ for all
$\varphi\in\mathbb{R}$. The set of such matrices is much smaller than
$\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$ --- and turns out to be exactly $\mathbb{C}$.

\begin{proposition}[Equivariant 2-channel kernels are complex scalars]
\label{prop:equivariant-subspace}
Let $W\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $W R_\varphi = R_\varphi W$ for all $\varphi\in\mathbb{R}$;
  \item $W = aI + bJ$ for some $a, b\in\mathbb{R}$;
  \item $W$ corresponds to multiplication by the complex scalar
    $a+ib$ under the standard $\mathbb{R}$-algebra embedding
    $\iota:\mathbb{C}\hookrightarrow M_2(\mathbb{R})$,
    $\iota(a+ib)=aI+bJ$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)$ is the definition of $\iota$.
$(2)\Rightarrow(1)$: $aI+bJ$ commutes with every polynomial in $J$,
including $R_\varphi=\exp(\varphi J)$. $(1)\Rightarrow(2)$:
differentiating $W R_\varphi = R_\varphi W$ at $\varphi=0$ gives
$WJ=JW$. Writing $W=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ and
expanding both sides forces $d=a$ and $c=-b$, so $W=aI+bJ$. (Equivalently:
$SO(2)$ acts irreducibly on $\mathbb{R}^2$, so by Schur's lemma the
real commutant is the division algebra
$\mathrm{End}_{SO(2)}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{C}$.)\qed
\end{proof}

\Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace} sharpens the "complex layers have an
inductive bias" intuition into a precise capacity statement.
A two-input-channel real linear map has $4$ free parameters per kernel
tap; a $U(1)$-equivariant one has only $2$, namely the coefficients
$(a,b)$ of $aI+bJ$. The complex layer is exactly the
$U(1)$-equivariant subspace of the corresponding stacked-real layer,
not a richer object: complex multiplication \emph{is} the equivariance
constraint, written in coordinates that make the constraint trivial
to satisfy. The matched-parameter real baseline of
\Cref{app:architectures} is therefore strictly more expressive than
the complex layer per tap, but it spends half of those parameters on
maps that violate the symmetry; if the task respects $U(1)$, those
extra parameters are at best wasted and at worst overfit the
asymmetry. This is the structural reason, beyond
\Cref{lem:phase-equivariance}, that complex layers can win at matched
parameters on phase-bearing data without offering any function the
real network could not in principle represent.

\paragraph{Relation to group-equivariant deep learning.}
\Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace} is the 1-D specialization of a
pattern that is now standard in geometric deep
learning~\cite{cohen2016group,bronstein2021geometric}: a group action
(here $U(1) = SO(2)$) on the input space induces a constraint on the
linear map's commutant; equivariance amounts to choosing parameters
inside that commutant. In two spatial dimensions, this is the
construction Worrall et al.~\cite{worrall2017harmonic} use in their
\emph{harmonic networks}, where complex-valued circular harmonics
parameterize $SO(2)$-equivariant 2-D convolutions; their layer is
mathematically the 2-D analogue of our \code{ComplexConv1d} viewed
through Prop.~\ref{prop:equivariant-subspace}, and they observe the
same generalization advantage on rotation-bearing tasks. Our paper's
contribution is not the equivariance construction itself, which is
classical, but the empirical mapping (\Cref{sec:representation-stress})
of when this inductive bias helps, hurts, or is irrelevant on
phase- and amplitude-bearing 1-D signal data, and the methodological
finding (\Cref{sec:selection-rules}) that some of the apparent
advantage in the literature is selection-rule-dependent rather than
structural.

This is the structural inductive bias the synthetic phase
classification result (\Cref{sec:phase-classification}), the RF
stress tests (\Cref{sec:representation-stress}), and the RadioML result
(\Cref{sec:radioml}) probe in complementary regimes. A single complex
sample (phase classification) gives no temporal structure for
convolutional equivariance to bite on, and the 2-D real network has
the same ``see real and imag'' feature view, so the complex network
buys nothing --- the null is what \Cref{lem:phase-equivariance}
predicts. A length-$L$ IQ sequence does have temporal structure, but
\Cref{sec:representation-stress} shows that temporal structure alone
is not enough to justify a universal claim: PSK rewards phase-aware
geometry, QAM rewards amplitude geometry, and fixed carrier-phase
rotation requires either augmentation or an explicit invariance
mechanism. The observed wins are consistent with the complex prior
when these conditions match, without requiring every activation in the
stack to preserve exact equivariance.

\paragraph{What the lemma does not explain.}
Phase equivariance of the \emph{layer} is necessary for the
architectural prior, but the activations between conv layers vary in
their own equivariance. By direct computation: \code{modrelu} and
\code{siglog} are phase-equivariant; \code{crelu}, \code{cardioid},
and \code{zrelu} are not. If the robustness asymmetry in
\Cref{sec:radioml} were \emph{driven} by activation phase equivariance, the
stable activations should be exactly \code{modrelu} and \code{siglog}.
They are not: \Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism} shows \code{modrelu} and
\code{zrelu} are stable ($0/9$ dead seeds across nine real-baseline
runs each), while \code{crelu}, \code{cardioid}, and \code{siglog} are
unstable ($3/9$ dead). One phase-equivariant activation is on each
side of the divide. The disambiguation in \Cref{sec:lr-vs-activation}
sharpens this further: when we swap learning rates between
\code{crelu} and \code{zrelu}, the dead-seed pattern follows the lr,
not the activation. \textbf{Phase equivariance of the layer is
necessary for the inductive bias, but the robustness asymmetry
\Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism} measures lives one level lower --- at
the optimization dynamics induced by $(\mathrm{Re}, \mathrm{Im})$
parameter coupling on the classifier head under high-learning-rate AdamW steps,
not at the equivariance of the activation.} The two effects are
partially independent and partially compounding. This also explains why the
\Cref{sec:radioml} result is most defensible at activations like
\code{zrelu} where complex wins by a small margin rather than at
\code{crelu} where the apparent gap is amplified by the
optimization-side mechanism.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Complex-valued neural networks.}
The mathematical foundations are laid out in Hirose's
textbook~\cite{hirose2012complex} and the multi-valued neuron
tradition of Aizenberg~\cite{aizenberg2011complex}. The modern deep
CVNN era anchors on Trabelsi et
al.~\cite{trabelsi2018deep} (\code{ComplexConv2d},
\code{ComplexBatchNorm}, the \code{CReLU}/\code{ZReLU}/\code{ModReLU}
activation triple, and a complex weight initialization),
Guberman~\cite{guberman2016complex} (early complex-valued CNN for
classification), and the unitary RNN line of
Arjovsky et al.~\cite{arjovsky2016unitary}, extended by Wisdom et
al.~\cite{wisdom2016fullcapacity} to full unitary capacity.
Our paper takes Trabelsi et al.\ as the canonical CVNN baseline
(activation set, default \code{CReLU}, magnitude readout) but reframes
the comparison protocol they used: we show that the matched-shared-trial
selection rule under their \code{CReLU} default is the regime in
which CVNN-vs-real gaps are most fragile, and we use \code{ZReLU} and
\code{ModReLU} as more conservative reference points.

\paragraph{Wirtinger / CR-calculus and complex backpropagation.}
Brandwood~\cite{brandwood1983complex} introduced the complex gradient
operator in adaptive array signal processing; the formalism was
popularized for ML practitioners by Kreutz-Delgado's
tutorial~\cite{kreutzdelgado2009complex}. PyTorch's complex
autograd~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} adopts the conjugate-Wirtinger
convention, which we make explicit in \Cref{sec:wirtinger-trilemma}
and verify empirically in our test suite (the rotation-equivariant
control of \Cref{sec:rotation-equivariant-control}). The Liouville
trilemma we state (\Cref{prop:trilemma}) is folklore, but to our
knowledge no prior CVNN paper presents it alongside an empirical
CR-residual measurement of each candidate activation as we do in
\Cref{sec:activation-characterization}.

\paragraph{Group-equivariant deep learning.}
The general construction of $G$-equivariant convolutional networks
was introduced by Cohen \& Welling~\cite{cohen2016group} for discrete
groups and extended to steerable~\cite{cohen2017steerable} and
general $E(2)$-equivariant~\cite{weiler2019general} CNNs; the broader
geometric DL framework is surveyed in Bronstein et
al.~\cite{bronstein2021geometric}. The closest 2-D analogue of our
\Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace} is the harmonic network of Worrall
et al.~\cite{worrall2017harmonic}, where complex-valued circular
harmonics parameterize $SO(2)$-equivariant 2-D convolutions. Our
Prop.~\ref{prop:equivariant-subspace} is the 1-D $U(1)$ specialization
of that pattern: it identifies the 1-D \code{ComplexConv1d} as the
$2$-parameter equivariant subspace of the unconstrained
$4$-parameter stacked-real Conv1d, which makes the Trabelsi-style
complex layer mathematically continuous with this lineage rather
than an unrelated construction.

\paragraph{Complex-valued networks for radio modulation classification.}
O'Shea et al.~\cite{oshea2018radioml} introduced the RadioML
2018.01A archive with a real-valued ResNet baseline reaching
$\sim$90\% top-1 at high SNR on the full 24-class task. Krzyston
et al.~\cite{krzyston2020complex} and Tu et al.~\cite{tu2020complex}
report complex-valued AMC architectures with a few-percentage-point
edge over real ResNets on the same setup. Our 3-class subset result
is consistent with this small advantage \emph{when activations are
stable and selection is independent per family}; the larger
matched-shared-trial gap we observe under \code{CReLU} is a
selection-protocol artifact (\Cref{sec:selection-rules}) rather than
a contradiction of the literature, and the per-step mechanism
analysis in \Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism} explains why such artifacts
can appear at all.

\paragraph{Complex-valued networks in physics and biomedical signals.}
CVNNs have been applied to MRI reconstruction with reported gains
over real architectures (Cole et al.~\cite{cole2021analysis}; Virtue
et al.~\cite{virtue2017better}). Physics-informed neural networks for
PDE solving (Raissi et al.~\cite{raissi2019pinns}) typically use
real-valued architectures, but the underlying physical fields can
be complex; our quantum-wavefunction pilot
(\Cref{sec:quantum-pilot}) is a small probe in that direction. EEG
deep learning has converged on real-valued architectures operating
on raw or magnitude-pooled signals (Schirrmeister et
al.~\cite{schirrmeister2017deep}, EEGNet~\cite{lawhern2018eegnet});
the analytic-signal representation we use in
\Cref{sec:eeg-pilot} is standard in the signal-processing literature
on phase locking and phase-amplitude
coupling~\cite{tort2010measuring}, but native complex-valued network
architectures remain uncommon in EEG. Our pilot is positioned as a
representation-protocol demonstration rather than a clinical claim.

\paragraph{Benchmarking methodology and the variance-in-evaluation literature.}
The selection-rule artifact we document
(\Cref{sec:selection-rules}) belongs to a now-familiar family of
findings in which apparent architectural advantages partly dissolve
under tighter evaluation protocols: Henderson et
al.~\cite{henderson2018deep} on deep RL, Lucic et
al.~\cite{lucic2018gans} on GANs, Melis et
al.~\cite{melis2018state} on language models. Bouthillier et
al.~\cite{bouthillier2021accounting} formalize the seed-vs.-hyperparameter
variance decomposition and recommend reporting both. Our
contribution to this literature is specific: we identify the
matched-shared-trial selection rule, used widely in CVNN papers as a
``capacity-fairness'' device, as the operational mechanism, and we
provide per-step gradient telemetry
(\Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism}, \Cref{app:telemetry}) that locates the
underlying instability at the readout's first AdamW step.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RadioML subset, not the full archive.} The headline
    real-data run used BPSK / QPSK / 8PSK at 8 SNR levels with
    \code{max\_per\_class\_per\_snr=256}. The full archive has 24
    modulations $\times$ 26 SNRs and a much larger per-bucket count.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic QAM is measured, RadioML QAM is not.} The
    stress tests include QAM16 / QAM64 and show that magnitude can
    dominate there, but the real-data RadioML mechanism study remains
    PSK-only. A full RadioML QAM/mixed-modulation run is required
    before making application claims about amplitude-heavy real RF
    classes.
  \item \textbf{No learned rotation invariance guarantee.} The fixed
    rotation stress test shows that neither complex layers nor
    phase-aware real features automatically solve unseen carrier-phase
    offsets under the current architecture. Rotation augmentation helps
    but does not make the problem disappear.
  \item \textbf{Application pilots are deliberately small.} The quantum
    wavefunction study uses 1-D synthetic states, a short split-step
    evolution, five hand-designed potential families, and three seeds.
    The EEG study uses synthetic analytic signals rather than clinical
    sensor data. These are evidence that the representation/stress-test
    protocol transfers outside RF, not evidence about production
    scientific or biomedical ML workloads.
  \item \textbf{Seed counts and inference.} The headline RadioML and
    synthetic RF sweeps use $n=6$ seeds; the representation stress
    tests, quantum and EEG pilots, and synthetic phase-classification
    sweep use $n=3$. Per-family $95\%$ CIs in $n=6$ tables now use
    the $t$-distribution ($t_{0.975, 5}=2.571$) rather than the
    Gaussian $1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ approximation; the $n=3$ pilot
    tables report means and per-condition stress-test outcomes
    without CIs since neither Gaussian nor bootstrap CIs are
    informative at that sample size, and we treat those tables as
    representation maps rather than significance tests. Where seeds
    are matched across families (matched-shared-trial selection), we
    use paired-$t$ tests on per-seed differences, which is both more
    powerful and more design-appropriate than the unpaired Welch
    approximation we used in an earlier draft. A bootstrap CI on six
    seeds gives widths similar to the $t$-CI and we do not report it
    separately.
  \item \textbf{One loss.} Classification runs use cross-entropy.
  \item \textbf{No \code{ComplexBatchNorm} or \code{ComplexLayerNorm}.}
    Landing them (in the form of Trabelsi et
    al.~\cite{trabelsi2018deep}'s whitening covariance) would let us
    train deeper conv stacks fairly.
  \item \textbf{Equivariance characterized at the layer level only.}
    \Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace} and the rotation-equivariant
    control of \Cref{sec:rotation-equivariant-control} establish
    equivariance per linear layer. Total network equivariance
    additionally requires equivariant activations and bias-free or
    equivariant biases (\Cref{app:phase-equivariance}); the
    fixed-rotation stress test (\Cref{sec:representation-stress})
    and the quantum global-phase shift result
    (\Cref{tab:quantum-pilot}) document the resulting gap empirically.
    A construction that is equivariant end-to-end --- e.g.\ stacks of
    \code{ComplexConv1d} with \code{ModReLU} or \code{Siglog} and
    bias-free linear readouts --- is a natural next step.
  \item \textbf{Modest hardware.} CPU and Apple Silicon for
    development; one A100 for the headline RF sweep. No
    multi-machine reproducibility check.
  \item \textbf{Learning-rate-vs-activation disambiguation is 2$\times$2.}
    \Cref{sec:lr-vs-activation} establishes the learning-rate-driven character
    of the dead-seed threshold using \code{crelu} (the largest gap)
    and \code{zrelu} (the smallest), but does not run the full
    factorial across all five activations. The cardioid and siglog
    rows in \Cref{tab:dead-seeds} are unaccounted for under this
    framing except by interpolation; a five-activation learning-rate sweep
    would close that gap.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale up the RadioML run} to the full
    24-modulation $\times$ 26-SNR archive on the full 1024-sample
    length. The infrastructure is in place
    (\code{sweep\_radioml.py --preset full}); the run is deferred from
    the headline write-up to keep the paper anchored on the 3-class
    subset that supports the mechanism analysis. Recommended first
    activation is \code{zrelu} --- the only ablation point where real
    baselines also train cleanly, so the comparison is least confounded
    by the matched-shared-trial selection's interaction with the
    explosion mechanism.
  \item \textbf{Port the representation stress suite to RadioML.}
    The synthetic stress tests are already the right scientific shape:
    PSK-only, QAM-only, mixed modulations, low/high SNR, amplitude
    normalization, carrier-phase rotation, augmentation, and activation
    ablation. The next defensible scale-up is to run the same grid on
    RadioML rather than only increasing class count.
  \item \code{ComplexBatchNorm} (whitening 2$\times$2 covariance, per
    Trabelsi et al.~\cite{trabelsi2018deep}) and deeper conv stacks.
  \item \textbf{Conv2d and 2-D complex domains.} Audio STFT, SAR,
    MRI~\cite{cole2021analysis,virtue2017better}, and spectrogram-like
    RF features are natural targets, but they require
    \code{ComplexConv2d}, normalization, and representation stress
    tests specific to 2-D phase/amplitude geometry. The 2-D analogue
    of \Cref{prop:equivariant-subspace} is the harmonic-network
    construction~\cite{worrall2017harmonic}, which gives a principled
    starting point.
  \item \textbf{Scale the physics pilot} from 1-D wavefunctions to
    richer PDE settings: 2-D wave packets, inverse scattering,
    Fourier-domain operators, and explicit gauge/global-phase
    invariant architectures.
  \item \textbf{Run the EEG pilot on real analytic-signal data:}
    sleep staging, seizure detection, brain-computer-interface motor
    imagery, or anesthesia depth are natural tasks where phase
    locking, amplitude envelopes, and phase-amplitude
    coupling~\cite{tort2010measuring} have existing signal-processing
    meaning. The natural baselines are
    EEGNet~\cite{lawhern2018eegnet} and
    Schirrmeister et al.~\cite{schirrmeister2017deep}, both of which
    operate on real-valued representations and would let us measure
    whether the analytic-signal CVNN view adds anything beyond
    well-engineered real architectures.
  \item A separate study of per-activation training dynamics: does
    \code{ModReLU}'s bias-sign regime visibly affect convergence?
\end{itemize}

artifacts:

\section{Phase equivariance: full proof and caveats}
\label{app:phase-equivariance}

\Cref{lem:phase-equivariance} states that a bias-free
\code{ComplexConv1d} layer commutes with global phase rotation, and
that the bias-bearing case picks up a controlled correction term. We
expand the proof and discuss what does and does not propagate through
the activation stack.

\begin{proposition}[Phase equivariance, bias-free]
\label{prop:phase-equivariance-biasfree}
Let $C : \mathbb{C}^{T} \to \mathbb{C}^{T}$ denote a one-dimensional
complex convolution with kernel $w \in \mathbb{C}^{K}$, no bias, and
zero padding (\code{padding\_mode='zeros'}). For any
$z \in \mathbb{C}^{T}$ and any global phase $\phi \in \mathbb{R}$:
\[
  C(e^{i\phi} z)[t] \;=\; e^{i\phi} \, C(z)[t]
  \qquad \forall t \in \{0, \ldots, T-1\}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By linearity over $\mathbb{C}$:
\[
  C(e^{i\phi} z)[t]
  = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w_k \cdot (e^{i\phi} z)[t-k]
  = e^{i\phi} \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} w_k \cdot z[t-k]
  = e^{i\phi} \, C(z)[t].
\]
The phase factor commutes through the sum because complex scalar
multiplication is commutative; the sum extracts it cleanly. Boundary
indices $t - k < 0$ contribute zero on both sides because zero padding
is itself phase-invariant ($e^{i\phi} \cdot 0 = 0$). \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Phase equivariance, bias-bearing]
\label{prop:phase-equivariance-bias}
With learnable bias $b \in \mathbb{C}$:
\[
  C(e^{i\phi} z)[t] \;=\; e^{i\phi} \, C(z)[t] \;+\; (1 - e^{i\phi}) \, b.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$C(e^{i\phi} z)[t] = e^{i\phi} (C(z)[t] - b) + b = e^{i\phi} C(z)[t] +
b - e^{i\phi} b$. \qed
\end{proof}

\paragraph{What survives the activation stack.} Phase equivariance is
preserved layer-by-layer if and only if the activation $\sigma$ itself
satisfies $\sigma(e^{i\phi} z) = e^{i\phi} \sigma(z)$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ModReLU} ($\sigma(z) = \mathrm{ReLU}(|z| + b) \cdot
    e^{i \angle z}$): equivariant. The magnitude term depends only on
    $|z|$, which is rotation-invariant; the phase factor passes through
    unchanged.
  \item \textbf{ComplexCardioid} ($\sigma(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}
    (1 + \cos \angle z) \cdot z$): \emph{not} equivariant in general,
    because the gate changes under rotation:
    $\cos(\angle z + \phi) \neq \cos(\angle z)$ for generic $\phi$.
  \item \textbf{Siglog} ($\sigma(z) = z / (1 + |z|)$): equivariant.
  \item \textbf{ComplexTanh} ($\sigma(z) = \tanh z$, the meromorphic
    extension): \emph{not} equivariant. Direct check: for $z = 1$ and
    $\phi = \pi/2$, $\tanh(iz) = \tanh(i) = i\tan 1 \approx 1.557\,i$
    while $e^{i\phi}\tanh(z) = i\tanh(1) \approx 0.762\,i$.
  \item \textbf{CReLU} ($\sigma(z) = \mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{Re}\,z)
    + i\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{Im}\,z)$): \emph{not} equivariant. A
    rotation $e^{i\phi}$ mixes real and imaginary components before
    the split, elementwise nonlinearity.
  \item \textbf{ZReLU} (zero out unless $\angle z \in [0, \pi/2]$): not
    equivariant (the kept-quadrant condition is rotation-sensitive).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{So four of our six characterized activations break equivariance.} The
empirical robustness asymmetry we report (a step-1
explosion-into-dead-region pattern that disproportionately hurts real
baselines) does \emph{not} track which activations preserve layer-level
phase equivariance. CReLU does not preserve equivariance but is the
configuration with the largest measured CVNN-vs-real gap; Siglog does
preserve equivariance but appears in the unstable high-learning-rate regime. We
therefore locate the asymmetry one level lower than the activation, in the
$(\mathrm{Re}, \mathrm{Im})$ parameter coupling that the optimizer sees
on \code{head.weight} at step 1; \Cref{sec:radioml-mechanism}
develops this in the body.

\paragraph{Why matched-shared-trial.} An independent winner per family
selects the family-best hyperparameter from a 16-trial search, which
biases up the best of four 16-trial searches and conflates capacity
allocation across families. The shared-trial rule fixes the trial
index across families, so each family is evaluated at the same
allocation; capacity matching is then transparent. We report
independent-selection numbers as a diagnostic
(\Cref{app:per-activation-results}); the gap between the two
selection rules is itself one of the artifacts the C\&F framing
exposes.

\section{Architecture details and parameter counts}
\label{app:architectures}

The four baseline families differ in capacity-allocation rule against
the complex network:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{complex}: \code{ComplexConv1d(2c$\to$h)} or
    \code{ComplexLinear(d$\to$h)}, complex weights \texttt{[h$\times$2c, K]} or
    \texttt{[h$\times$d]} with 2 floats/parameter (real and imaginary).
  \item \code{real\_stacked}: \code{Conv1d(2$\to$h)} or
    \code{Linear(2d$\to$h)}; treats $(I, Q)$ as 2 input channels.
    Same $h$ as the complex network, half the parameter count
    (1 float per weight).
  \item \code{real\_matched\_params}: hidden width chosen to match
    parameter count to the complex family. For a complex
    \code{(2c $\to$ h, K)} layer with $2chK$ complex weights = $4chK$
    floats, the matched real layer is \code{(2 $\to$ 2h, K)} = $4hK$
    floats, identical at $c=1$ but scaling to $\sqrt{2} h$ at $c > 1$.
  \item \code{real\_matched\_flops}: hidden width chosen to match
    forward-pass multiply-accumulate count rather than parameter count.
    For our 1-D conv layers the resulting widths track
    \code{matched\_params} closely (within 5\%); listed separately to
    avoid silently conflating ``capacity'' senses.
\end{itemize}

The synthetic phase classifier uses a 1-layer MLP head (no conv); the
RF/RadioML classifiers use \code{ComplexConv1d} (or \code{Conv1d} on
$(I,Q)$ stacked channels) followed by global magnitude pool + linear
readout. Magnitude pool is $|z|$ per channel for the complex family
and identity for real, since the real channels are already real-valued
at the readout.

\section{Gradient telemetry: per-layer, per-step}
\label{app:telemetry}

Telemetry instruments every parameter tensor with per-step
$\|\nabla_\theta L\|_F$, the Cauchy-Riemann residual on complex
parameters (zero by construction for the complex family), and an
intrinsic Jacobian-norm proxy. Runs cover 5 activations
$\times$ 4 families $\times$ 3 seeds = 60 instrumented trajectories,
each capped at 200 steps for tractability on commodity hardware.

\paragraph{Step-1 head-gradient ratio.} Across all five activations,
the step-1 ratio
$\|\nabla_{\text{head.weight}} L\|_{\text{real}} \,/\,
\|\nabla_{\text{head.weight}} L\|_{\text{complex}}$
falls into two regimes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gap-bearing} (CReLU, ComplexCardioid, Siglog): ratio
    in the range $50$--$80$ at step 1; collapses to $\sim 1$ by step
    20 for surviving seeds, but seeds whose post-step-1 head landed in
    a dead-ReLU region never produce gradient signal again.
  \item \textbf{Stable} (ModReLU, ZReLU): ratio in the range
    $1.5$--$3$ at step 1; no seeds enter dead-loss states.
\end{itemize}

The grouping is consistent across seeds and across the synthetic-data
replication (\Cref{app:synthetic-replication}). The
$50$--$80\times$ figure is a step-1 measurement on raw weights with
fan-in initialization; later steps post-Adam-warmup show smaller
ratios under all activations. The point of measuring at step 1 is that
that is where the dead seeds become dead; past step 1, the parameter
state has already diverged.

\paragraph{Why \code{head.weight}, specifically.} The conv stack's
parameters show no comparable real-vs-complex ratio at any step. The
explosion is a property of how matched-trial hyperparameter selection
maps into the readout when the readout sees magnitude-pooled inputs
(complex) versus stacked $(\mathrm{Re}, \mathrm{Im})$ inputs (real).
The complex magnitude pool produces inputs of comparable scale across
seeds; the stacked $(\mathrm{Re}, \mathrm{Im})$ readout sees inputs
whose scale is seed-dependent through the conv-stack random init, and
high-learning-rate trials interact badly with the unlucky tail of that
distribution.

\section{Synthetic-data replication of the threshold mechanism}
\label{app:synthetic-replication}

To rule out RadioML-specific effects (CFO, multipath, pulse shaping)
as the cause of the explosion, we re-run the gradient telemetry
harness on synthetic AWGN-only IQ data with the same 4-family / 5-act
sweep and the same matched-trial hyperparameters. The synthetic data
generator is \code{experiments/rf/synthetic\_modulation.py}, which
produces raw-symbol $(I, Q)$ sequences with per-class label imbalance
matched to RadioML.

\paragraph{Result.} The dead-seed pattern replicates: CReLU,
ComplexCardioid, and Siglog produce 2--3 dead seeds per 9 in the real
families; ModReLU and ZReLU produce zero. The step-1 head-gradient
ratio sits in the same $50$--$80\times$ band for the gap-bearing
activations. We conclude the mechanism is hyperparameter-driven,
mediated by the readout's input distribution under matched
shared-trial selection, and not an artifact of RadioML's distortions.

\section{RadioML 2018.01A loader: aliases and strict-SNR mode}
\label{app:radioml-loader}

\paragraph{Class-label aliasing.} The original
\code{classes.txt} distributed with RadioML 2018.01A lists the 24
modulations in an order that does not match the one-hot ordering of
the \code{Y} array; this is a known issue in the community.
\code{experiments/rf/radioml.py} normalizes class labels through a
canonical map and prefers \code{classes-fixed.json} or
\code{classes-fixed.txt} sidecars next to the HDF5 file when present.
Common aliases accepted: \code{PSK8}~$\to$~\code{8PSK},
\code{QAM16}~$\to$~\code{16QAM}, \code{APSK16}~$\to$~\code{16APSK},
\code{ASK4}~$\to$~\code{4ASK}, AM labels with underscores
(\code{AM\_SSB\_WC}~$\to$~\code{AM-SSB-WC}), etc.

\paragraph{Strict-SNR mode.} Earlier loader versions silently dropped
samples at SNR levels not in the user's filter list. The default is
now \code{strict\_snr=True}, which raises rather than dropping; the
test suite includes a regression case that asserts on the strict
behaviour. The previous silent-drop behaviour is recoverable via
\code{strict\_snr=False} for compatibility with downstream code that
intentionally subsets.

\section{Reproducibility: manifest schema and CI guards}
\label{app:reproducibility}

Every long-running script writes a JSON manifest into the run's output
directory (under \code{results/}). Manifest fields:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{git\_commit}: the SHA at run start.
  \item \code{git\_dirty}: boolean, computed on a tree filtered to
    exclude \code{results/} and the activation-characterization output
    directory (otherwise regenerating figures from a clean checkout
    would flip the bit on its own outputs).
  \item \code{python}, \code{torch}, \code{numpy} versions.
  \item \code{cuda}: device string and CUDA version, or
    \code{"cpu"}.
  \item \code{argv}: full command line.
  \item \code{seed}, \code{n\_trials}, \code{search\_space}: the
    hyperparameter regime.
  \item \code{wall\_clock\_seconds}: end-to-end runtime.
\end{itemize}

CI surfaces newly-added or modified manifests with
\code{git\_dirty: true} as warnings on the PR (initially a hard fail;
loosened to a warning at the request of practical iteration). A
structural CI guard checks the committed activation characterization
artifact contains all six expected activation rows after a regen, so
that figure regeneration cannot silently drop an activation.

\paragraph{Caching.} Capped sweep subsets cache the seed/filter split
to disk and reuse it across trial/family/seed runs. Uncapped
full-archive runs disable caching automatically (the working set
exceeds typical Mac SSD scratch space); pass \code{--no-cache-data}
to force-disable for capped runs that nevertheless overflow memory.

\section{Full-archive RadioML scale-up: cost and deferred plan}
\label{app:scale-up}

The submitted experiments are at \code{--preset subset}: 3 PSK
modulations, 8 even SNR levels, \code{max\_per\_class\_per\_snr=256},
\code{sample\_length=128}. The infrastructure for full-archive runs is
in place via \code{--preset full} (24 modulations, 26 SNR levels,
\code{sample\_length=1024}); we have deliberately deferred running it
because the mechanism analysis is the contribution and the scale-up
risks scope creep relative to a C\&F submission.

\paragraph{Cost estimate.} A single
\code{--preset full --activation crelu --seeds 0 1 2} run on an A100
40GB takes 5--15 hours depending on which trial samples land at the
upper end of the search space (the search box's largest configuration
is the bottleneck). Six activations $\times$ 3 seeds $\times$ 16
trials $\times$ 4 families = 1152 instrumented runs at full scale; in
practice we would run 2--3 activations (CReLU as the headline,
ZReLU as the stable counter-example, and one of
\{ComplexCardioid, Siglog\} as a disambiguation). On 4$\times$A100 in
parallel, $\sim$24 hours wall-clock for a 3-activation full sweep is a
realistic estimate.

\paragraph{What to expect at scale.} The C\&F prediction is that the
two-effect decomposition holds: the architectural prior (phase
equivariance, magnitude readout) survives at 24-class /
1024-sample scale, and the optimization-level robustness asymmetry
remains hyperparameter-driven and disappears under independent per-family
selection. We do not predict the specific numerical gap because the
full-archive distribution is qualitatively different from the subset
(more distortion-heavy modulations like AM-SSB-WC and FM are present
at low SNR), and the matched-trial selection rule may admit different
hyperparameter regimes once the conv stack has more channels.

\paragraph{Failure modes to watch for.} (1) Caching off may surface
stragglers if the loader's \code{strict\_snr=True} default flags
HDF5 SNR levels not in the user's filter; the loader's regression
test covers this on the subset but the full archive has not been run
in CI. (2) A 24-class softmax has higher entropy at chance ($\ln 24
\approx 3.18$ vs $\ln 3 \approx 1.10$), so the dead-loss threshold for
detecting failed seeds shifts; the telemetry harness already infers
this from the dataset's class count.
